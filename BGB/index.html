Katalin Cseh-Varga and Adam Czirak, eds. The Mediated Plant. e-flux journal, 2018. On the nonhuman, the vegetal, and what it means to sense without a nervous system.
What does it mean to mediate a plant? The question is not merely technological — it is ontological. To place a camera before a fern, to attach electrodes to a leaf, to translate the slow upward arc of a heliotropic stem into data, is to stage an encounter between two radically different temporalities: the human and the vegetal. Plants do not think. Or rather, they think differently — distributed across root networks, expressed through chemical signals, enacted over durations that exceed human attention. The mediated plant is always already a translation, a transformation, a betrayal of the original slowness. The essay asks: when we make plants visible through technology, do we understand them better — or do we merely project our own desires for legibility onto forms of life that resist it?
On plant perception
Monica Gagliano's acoustic experiments revealed that plant roots grow toward the sound of water — even when no water is present, only vibration. This is sensing without nerves, recognition without a brain. A form of intelligence so alien to us that we had to invent new instruments merely to witness it. The camera becomes a prosthesis. The microphone, a translator. Each device mediates not just the plant's behavior but our capacity to receive it. We are always reading the machine's reading of the plant — a double mediation that renders the original signal further and further from reach. What the apparatus shows us is not the plant sensing — it is the plant translated into something we can sense. The gap between these two things is where all the interesting questions live.
Biofeedback and the body
Experiments in plant biofeedback — attaching galvanic sensors to leaves, monitoring electrical changes in response to human proximity — collapse the boundary between organism and instrument. The plant becomes a kind of readout; the human, an event in its environment. This reversal is significant. We usually position ourselves as the observers, the analysts, the ones who read the world. But in these setups, the plant reads us. Our presence registers as a disturbance in its electrical field. We become the signal in someone else's data.
Technology as translation
Every image of a plant is already a mediation. The herbarium specimen, pressed and dried, translates living tissue into archival matter. The botanical illustration, however precise, translates three-dimensional growth into two-dimensional representation. The time-lapse film translates vegetal duration into human-speed cinema. What changes with digital mediation is not the fact of translation but its velocity and its volume. We can now produce images of plants faster than plants can grow. We can generate more representations of a leaf than there are leaves on the plant. The archive threatens to overwhelm the organism it documents. This excess of mediation is itself a form of violence — not intentional, but structural. The plant disappears beneath its representations, replaced by data, by spectacle, by the smooth surfaces of a screen.
The apparatus of visibility
What kinds of plants become visible through technology, and which remain invisible? The photogenic specimen, the rare orchid, the carnivorous novelty — these are the plants that circulate through media. The weed, the grass, the unremarkable fern — these are plants whose lives pass entirely without technological witness. Visibility is always political. The question of which organisms we choose to mediate — to make legible, to amplify, to grant technological attention — is also a question about which forms of life we value, which we study, which we protect.
Vegetal duration
Time-lapse photography was developed precisely to make plant movement visible to human eyes. In real time, a sunflower tracking the arc of the sun, a tendril spiraling toward a support, a seed cracking open and pushing upward through soil — these are all too slow for human perception. The camera speeds them up, compresses vegetal time into human time. But something is lost in this acceleration. The actual experience of plant growth — the slowness, the persistence, the indifference to our impatience — is exactly what the time-lapse erases. We see the movement, but we do not feel the duration. We receive the conclusion without living through the process. Slowness, in this sense, is not a deficiency in plants. It is a different relationship to time — one that our technologies systematically override in order to make the vegetal legible to beings who live at a different speed.
Rhythm and interval
Plants have circadian rhythms. They open and close on schedules calibrated to light and temperature. They produce chemicals in response to seasons, to drought, to the presence of herbivores. They communicate through the soil — through fungal networks that carry signals across distances. These rhythms operate at scales that neither fully align with nor fully diverge from human rhythms. They are close enough to be recognizable, distant enough to be strange. The mediated plant — caught in time-lapse, monitored by sensor — is always being translated into our rhythm, our interval, our sense of what counts as an event.
Toward the light
Phototropism — the bending of plants toward light — is perhaps the most legible form of plant behavior. It is movement organized by desire, or at least by what functions like desire: a differential sensitivity to light that causes cells on one side to elongate faster than cells on the other, producing a curve, a reaching, an orientation. What would it mean to design an interface that bends toward the user the way a plant bends toward light? Not instantly, not mechanically, but slowly — adjusting over time, responding to presence rather than to command. An interface that senses you without demanding your input. This is the speculative design question the essay opens up: not just how to represent plant intelligence, but how to embody it — how to build systems that operate by its logic rather than merely depicting it.
Gravitropism and root logic
Roots grow downward through gravitropism — a response to gravity that requires no knowledge of direction, only a sensitivity to force. The root does not know it is going down. It simply responds, cell by cell, to the conditions it encounters. This is a model of navigation without a map, growth without intention, intelligence without consciousness. It challenges every assumption we make about how knowing and doing relate to each other — and suggests that some of the most successful forms of problem-solving in the world operate entirely without a mind.
The wood wide web
Beneath a forest floor, a network. Mycorrhizal fungi thread through soil, connecting root systems of different trees, enabling the transfer of carbon, water, and chemical signals across distances of many meters. Suzanne Simard's research demonstrated that trees use this network to share resources — that a mother tree can send carbon to her seedlings growing in shade, that trees can warn their neighbors of insect attack. This is a rhizomatic system in the Deleuzian sense: decentralized, without hierarchy, organized by connection rather than command. There is no brain, no center, no authority — only the network itself, and the flows that pass through it. The internet was once described with similar metaphors. But the internet has nodes and routers and servers — centers of a kind, hierarchies that emerged from apparent flatness. The mycorrhizal network resists this logic. It is genuinely distributed in ways that our technological networks only approximate.
Toward a vegetal interface
What would it mean to build a website that grows? Not one that loads quickly and presents information efficiently, but one that reveals itself slowly, that changes with each visit, that responds to the time of day, the weather at your location, the season in which you encounter it? The mediated plant suggests an alternative model for digital design — one organized not around the user's convenience but around the plant's temporality. An interface that asks you to wait. That does not complete itself. That leaves some things permanently out of reach. This website is that experiment. Content valid until conditions change. The site decays.
